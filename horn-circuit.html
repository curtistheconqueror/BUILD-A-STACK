HOW IT WORKS

  1. You press the plastic cover — it pushes the brass cap down against the spring.
  2. The cap lands on the brass ring: the horn switch is now closed.
  3. A small current flows from the battery through the relay's coil, up the column wire, across the brass roller wheel onto the spinning brass plate, up to the ring — then back to ground through the spring, hub, and steering shaft. Because the roller rides on the plate, this works at any steering angle.
  4. The energized coil snaps the relay's armature shut — click.
  5. Full battery power now flows through the relay's 30 → 87 contacts and down the horn power wire.
  6. A splice feeds both horns — high note and low note together.
  7. Each horn returns its current to ground two ways: through its mounting bracket on the frame, and through its redundant ground wire. The frame carries it all back to the battery's negative post.

Diagram is schematic — the geometry is simplified, but the circuit order matches the real setup: button → slip-ring contact → relay → twin horns, with the horns switched on the power side and permanently grounded.

Before you press anything, part of this circuit is already electrically hot — full battery voltage sitting and waiting at an open switch, with zero current moving and no work being done. That's potential, not current — a meter would read 12 V there, even though nothing is "happening." Toggle ⚡ POTENTIAL above to see exactly how far that resting voltage reaches before the button (or a fault) stops it.

FAULTS & DIAGNOSIS

A relay circuit has two sides. The control side — the coil, plus the button and slip-ring that trigger it — is a tiny current that only tells the relay what to do. The load side — the relay's contacts out to the horns — carries the real power. The relay is the power router sitting on the boundary, which makes the click your single most useful clue.

A click means the whole control side works — it energized the coil and threw the contacts — so it rules out everything upstream and sends you downstream to the load side. No click means you never got that far: the fault is upstream, on the control side. Pick a scenario above and press the horn to watch where the current stops.
